## Weekly updates

Every Friday, write a short update in your Project Landing Page, then send it as a brief email to the lab list with a link back to the doc. The doc is the archive; the email is the broadcast. Focus on what was accomplished or *learned*, not what was worked on. The template (traffic-light status adapted from Ben Kuhn's [notes on project management](https://www.benkuhn.net/pjm/)):

### Week of [date]

* **Status:** 🟢 on track / 🟡 at risk / 🔴 blocked
* **What changed / what we learned:** two to four bullets of key findings or decisions.
* **Up next:** the next de-risking step.
* **Landing page:** [link]

Put the traffic light in the email's subject line (e.g., `[Project name] — week of [date] 🟢`) — it lets everyone triage the inbox at a glance, and a 🔴 subject gets a same-day response. And remember the bullets state what was *learned*, not what was worked on: "ran simulations" is not an update; "the estimator is biased when cluster sizes are informative" is.

Keep the email under ~150 words. If it takes longer than ten minutes to write, you're writing too much.

## Slack vs. email

* **Slack — rapid iteration.** Quick clarifications, daily coordination, unblocking. Rule of thumb: if it can be resolved in under five minutes or unblocks you immediately, it goes in Slack. Post in the project-specific channel rather than DMs, so the whole team benefits from the shared context.
* **Email — formal and external.** External collaborators, grant logistics, scheduling, university administrative matters, and the Friday broadcast above.

## 1:1 meetings

These are for strategy and for discussing the *implications* of your results — not for walking through raw output. Come with a short agenda and an updated Project Landing Page. If your RMarkdown or notebook is clean, we spend the hour on what the results mean; if it isn't, we spend it untangling, which is a worse use of both of our time.
